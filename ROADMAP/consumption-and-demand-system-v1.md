# Mandate — Consumption & Demand System v1

> Status: planning draft  
> Scope: first playable demand/consumption layer  
> Goal: create believable economic demand without overcomplicating the early implementation

## 1. Core principle

Goods should have **real destinations and uses** in the economy.

Demand must not be a vague abstract modifier.

A good can be demanded because it is needed by:

- population;
- production;
- construction;
- infrastructure;
- the state;
- the military;
- maintenance;
- stockpiles.

The first playable demand system should remain compact and generic.

---

## 2. Total demand model

For early versions, total demand can be understood as:

```text
population needs
+
production inputs
+
construction / infrastructure
+
state / military demand
+
maintenance / replacement
+
stockpiles
=
total demand
```

The exact formulas can be added later.

The important thing is that each source of demand exists for a clear simulation reason.

---

## 3. Population needs

Population creates recurring demand for basic goods.

The most important categories are:

### Essential needs

- food;
- heating / fuel where needed;
- basic textiles / clothing.

Food is mandatory for settlement survival and population support.

Insufficient essential supply can later affect:

- health;
- mortality;
- migration;
- unrest;
- productivity;
- population growth.

### Ordinary consumption

Once essentials are covered, households can demand more goods such as:

- household goods;
- better clothing;
- paper / printed goods;
- tools and simple manufactured goods.

### Luxury consumption

Wealthier groups can create additional demand for:

- luxury goods;
- fine textiles;
- jewelry;
- expensive furniture;
- decorative goods;
- other high-status consumption.

The first implementation does not need a detailed household budget model.

A simple hierarchy is enough:

```text
essential
→ ordinary
→ luxury
```

---

## 4. Production demand

Production facilities consume goods as inputs.

Examples:

```text
ore + fuel
→ metallurgy
→ basic metals
```

```text
textile fibers
→ workshop / manufactory
→ textiles
```

```text
timber + metal goods
→ shipyard
→ ships / transport equipment
```

This demand is generated by production recipes.

A producer should only demand inputs that it actually needs.

---

## 5. Construction and infrastructure demand

Construction should consume physical goods and materials.

Examples:

- timber;
- construction materials;
- metals;
- glass;
- machinery;
- later electrical goods.

Possible consumers include:

- housing;
- workshops;
- manufactories;
- mines;
- ports;
- roads;
- canals;
- railways;
- power grids;
- state buildings;
- military infrastructure.

The important distinction is:

> A settlement does not simply "consume metal."

Instead, construction and infrastructure projects create demand for metal and other materials.

---

## 6. Capital stock and durable goods

Not all goods disappear when they are used.

Some become long-lived physical capital.

Examples:

- tools;
- machinery;
- rail;
- ships;
- vehicles;
- buildings;
- electrical infrastructure;
- industrial equipment.

These goods become:

```text
stock in use
```

They remain physically present for some period of time.

Later they can:

- degrade;
- require maintenance;
- be repaired;
- be replaced;
- be dismantled;
- become scrap;
- enter recycling.

---

## 7. State and military demand

The state can create its own demand.

Examples:

- food for armies;
- weapons;
- uniforms;
- ships;
- transport equipment;
- fuel;
- construction materials;
- fortifications;
- administrative supplies;
- strategic stockpiles.

This demand should come from actual government activity rather than arbitrary modifiers.

---

## 8. Maintenance and replacement demand

Existing infrastructure, buildings, equipment and vehicles should not remain perfect forever.

They can require:

- spare materials;
- replacement parts;
- fuel;
- tools;
- periodic rebuilding.

This creates ongoing demand even when no new construction is happening.

Detailed depreciation formulas are deferred.

---

## 9. Stockpiles

Goods do not need to be consumed immediately.

Possible stockpile holders include:

- households;
- enterprises;
- governments;
- armies;
- warehouses.

Stockpiles can later matter for:

- war;
- famine;
- trade disruption;
- price stabilization;
- strategic reserves.

Early versions can keep this simple.

---

## 10. Two broad kinds of use

A crucial distinction:

### 10.1 Flow consumption

The good is physically used up or transformed so strongly that it no longer remains available as the same material stock.

Examples:

```text
food
→ eaten

coal
→ burned

natural gas
→ burned
```

These are recurring flows.

### 10.2 Stock in use

The good becomes part of a durable object.

Examples:

```text
steel
→ railway rail
→ exists for decades
→ maintenance
→ dismantling
→ scrap
```

```text
copper
→ electrical grid
→ stock in use
→ later recovered as scrap
```

This connects directly to the planned material lifecycle and recycling system.

---

## 11. Relationship to settlements

Settlements are places where demand occurs.

They should not themselves be magical consumers of every good.

Instead, demand inside a settlement comes from:

- residents;
- enterprises;
- construction projects;
- infrastructure;
- government activity;
- military presence;
- storage.

This keeps demand physically explainable.

---

## 12. Population class differences

The first version can use a simple hierarchy.

A poor household prioritizes:

```text
food
→ heating / fuel
→ basic clothing
```

A wealthier household can additionally demand:

```text
better goods
→ household goods
→ paper / culture goods
→ luxury goods
```

The exact consumption baskets by stratum can be added later.

The current goal is to make wealth affect the **type and quantity of demand** without building a full consumer-choice model immediately.

---

## 13. Connection to markets

This demand system provides the demand side for later market mechanics.

Conceptually:

```text
population needs
+ production inputs
+ construction
+ government
+ maintenance
+ stockpiles
↓
local demand
↓
compare with supply
↓
shortage / surplus
↓
price response
```

Market-clearing and pricing formulas are separate future work.

---

## 14. Decisions considered settled for v1

1. Demand must come from real economic uses.
2. Food is an essential recurring need.
3. Population demand should initially use simple essential / ordinary / luxury tiers.
4. Production creates demand through recipe inputs.
5. Construction and infrastructure consume materials.
6. Settlements are locations of demand, not generic material sinks.
7. Durable goods can become stock in use instead of disappearing.
8. Government and military activity create their own demand.
9. Maintenance and replacement create ongoing material demand.
10. Stockpiles should exist conceptually even if implemented simply at first.
11. Flow consumption and stock-in-use consumption must remain distinct.
12. The demand system should integrate later with waste, scrap and recycling.
13. Early versions should prioritize clear simulation logic over detailed household microeconomics.

---

## 15. Intentionally deferred

Not yet fixed:

- exact consumption quantities;
- nutrition values;
- detailed household budgets;
- substitution between goods;
- exact stratum consumption baskets;
- rationing;
- famine mechanics;
- maintenance rates;
- depreciation formulas;
- exact stockpile behavior;
- warehouse capacity;
- spoilage;
- military logistics detail;
- government procurement rules;
- exact price formation.

These can be designed when the implementation reaches them.
